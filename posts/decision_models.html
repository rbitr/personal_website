Old risks for new decision models

Andrew Marble
marble.onl
andrew@willows.ai
October 13, 2026

Jev1 markets itself as a “System 1 AI Model”, an AI system providing “typed decisions with calibrated probabilities, so your software can account for uncertainty”. Concretely, it’s a classifier built on top of an LLM.

AI classifiers predate modern LLMs. A typical text classifier might tell you whether a short passage expresses positive or negative sentiment; they typically return a percent score for each possibility: “Great job!” might get rated 95% confident as positive sentiment, “great job.” might be 50/50 with punctuation and capitalization suggesting sarcasm. AI models are good at learning this nuance after seeing enough examples. General question answering can also be formulated as a classifier: What’s the capital of France? a) Prague; b) Nantes; c) Paris; d) Geneva. A classifier might return a) 2%, b) 15%, c) 88% d) 5%. Taking the one with the highest probability assigned by the classifier gives us its answer.

Recently classifiers have been rediscovered or reinvented as “decision models” like Jev. Decision models take a state such as a text passage, and a set of questions that can either be answered by a multiple choice, levels, or a yes/no probability. These models have the advantage of being based on LLMs and so have LLM-like flexibility and broad knowledge. While an older text classifier might need to be trained for a single specific task using a purpose-built labeled data set, decision models can be applied to many tasks without training. Compared to language models, decision models are very fast because they only need to make a single decision rather than generate a whole text passage to explain themselves.

Typical applications proposed since Jev came out in September 2026 are resume screening2, support ticket triage, guardrails3 - including screening risky tool calls4, etc. Many of these decisions can be consequential, and researchers have begun to explore the reliability of decision models, in particular their ability to resist misuse.

Compared to LLMs, the attack surface of decision models is smaller. Classifiers can't generate the inappropriate or dangerous output that LLM safety evaluations are normally concerned with. There are LLM evaluations such as WMDP5 that formulate cyber and biological capability as multiple choice questions. But even if a decision model scored well on such a benchmark, their usefulness would be very limited, and typical decision models are based on smaller LLMs and unlikely to perform at the frontier.

Bias is an important consideration when dealing with decision making. Especially in applications like resume screening, there’s a risk that inappropriate bias present in the decision model could influence choices. A recent bias benchmark6 has been updated to include decision models, and shows a decision model (“solar-decide”) in the top spot at 99.4% followed by GPT-Astra at 98.4%. Jev scores 83.8% on this benchmark. Avoiding bias is the responsibility of deployers to address at the architecture level, for example by ensuring the model is used for objective decisions (“does this person have 5 years’ experience”) rather than subjective (“pick which candidate is better for the job”) where bias can leak through7.

Perhaps the most concerning threat for decision models are inputs that deliberately manipulate the decision they make. The threat model here is that an input message (state) is crafted so as to skew the outcome in the attacker’s favor while avoiding notice in human review. In normal LLM parlance this would be (indirect) prompt injection. The history of adversarially attacking classifiers is rich, with research from 2014 demonstrating how images could be imperceptibly altered to appear normal to a human but be misclassified by a neural network8. Such manipulations could defeat guardrails or safety measures to allow an attack, or directly skew the outcome for example in the resume screening case. The threat is relevant whenever untrusted inputs are being screened through a decision model so could apply to most public facing deployments.

Initial research has considered both porting LLM-style prompt injections over to decision models and older adversarial classifier manipulations that also fit under prompt injection. Wu and Lim9 looked at using LLM style injections (“ignore previous instructions”, “important message”) designed to hijack an LLM conversation and adversarially manipulate it. They found these attacks ineffective though a variant where prompts were iteratively optimized could flip close decisions. On the other hand Wang et al. found that including information (rather than directions) that previous context was outdated followed by new information supporting the desired decision had success rates as high as 100%10.

Sun et al.11 found that category names were more important than definitions and that decisions could be flipped when names were swapped and definitions held constant. This suggests the model weights shallow lexical cues in the option spec higher than the stated semantics and thus may be subject to “keyword stuffing” style manipulation where the state text is filled with keywords suggesting the desired option.

In general, the more of the state an attacker controls the higher the chances of successfully flipping a decision. However this may also result in higher chances of the attack being discovered: a keyword stuffed resume may be obvious upon review. Decision models have also been shown to be susceptible to more subtle manipulation in which tokens or suffixes are specifically chosen to manipulate the class prediction probabilities without semantically altering the text. Such attacks were common with earlier generation classifiers. Wang et al referenced above showed a 100% attack success rate at shifting decisions using an adversarial suffix appended to text, constrained with a fluency penalty to appear readable.

While Jev is a closed model, open source variants exist. Whether open or closed, a key aspect of these models is that they reveal the probabilities of different decisions as a necessary part of their operation. These probabilities become the goal function against which an attacker can optimize and thus allow iterative techniques that hone in on a successful attack, rather than more chance-based attacks that target closed LLMs.

Beyond prompt injection, decision models may present other attack surfaces: their proliferation (including over 60 open source models at a recent count) could lead to supply chain attacks where purposefully manipulated models are released. Additionally, the probabilities or labels returned may be used to infer private information a model can access (Wang et al).

While the idea of classifiers is old, widespread interest in decision models is new. It’s expected that additional vulnerabilities will be identified and also that additional measures will be put in place by providers to counteract misuse. The speed of the models and visible output probabilities are both their biggest strengths and weaknesses. Where feasible, deployers can take steps including

  • rate-limiting

  • hiding output probabilities or adding noise to prevent iterative optimization attacks

  • reviewing close decisions and not simply relying on which probability is the highest in order to avoid flips

  • ensuring red-teaming takes into account the nature of these models rather than treating them as an LLM

  • capping the untrusted share of the state by wrapping untrusted text in trusted context

Jev is only about a month old as of writing and the results referenced here pertain to early versions of the model and API version (1.13). Updates may change some of the findings.
